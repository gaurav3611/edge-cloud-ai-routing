# II. Background and Related Work

**Categorized parallel inference at the edge.** EPARA [@epara2025] categorizes inference tasks along two axes and applies five operators accordingly: batching, multi-task co-location, model parallelism, multi-frame batching and request-level data parallelism. It couples this allocator with a decentralized request handler that offloads a request to a peer with probability proportional to the peer's idle goodput, a submodular service-placement algorithm with a $1/(1+P)$ guarantee [@fisher1978submodular], and ring-based state synchronization. Its handler, like those of the edge systems it compares against (InterEdge [@interedge], Galaxy [@galaxy], SERV-P [@servp]) and of datacenter serving systems (AlpaServe [@alpaserve], USHER [@usher], Nexus [@nexus], SHEPHERD [@shepherd], Clockwork [@clockwork], INFaaS [@infaas]), treats every server that hosts a model as a valid destination. We study EPARA's *request-handling* layer. Its operators are not modelled mechanistically: we reduce each category to a fixed slot and service-time profile (Section V), so batching gains and co-location interference are outside the scope of this paper.

**Edge-cloud collaborative intelligence.** A recent survey [@liu2025eccc] organizes ECCC research into model optimization, AI-driven resource management and privacy/security enhancement, and names heterogeneity management and real-time scheduling as open challenges. Model partitioning between device and cloud [@neurosurgeon] and collaborative edge LLM inference [@edgeshard] address *where to compute a model*; we address *where a request may be served*.

**Multilevel security and data sovereignty.** Eq. (1) is a two-attribute instance of multilevel-security labelling. The Bell–LaPadula model [@bell1973] assigns subjects and objects levels from an ordered set and forbids a subject from reading data above its clearance; Denning's lattice model [@denning1976] generalizes this to a lattice of security classes along which information may flow only upward. Our clearance levels form such an ordered set, and the trust-domain condition for the highest sensitivity adds a compartment, so the eligible destinations of a request are exactly the servers whose (clearance, domain) label dominates the request's label. Data-sovereignty work [@hummel2021] and regulation such as the GDPR [@gdpr] motivate the domain compartment: some data may be processed only under the control of a particular organization or jurisdiction. We do not contribute to the theory of these models; we use their simplest form as a hard constraint inside a fast decentralized router and measure what it costs.

**Security- and privacy-aware fog and edge scheduling.** The closest prior work classifies tasks by privacy level and restricts where they may run. Singh et al. [@singh2021fog] divide fog applications into public, semi-private and private classes that must be hosted on untrusted, semi-trusted and trusted resources, respectively, and place interactive and batch tasks with a distributed orchestrator that weighs performance against security requirements. PASHE [@fizza2018pashe] schedules private, semi-private and public real-time jobs across edge micro data centers and the cloud, executing private jobs with tight deadlines on the user's local micro data center. Our clearance levels and sensitivities follow the same idea. The differences are in the setting and the questions asked: we route individual requests of categorized GPU inference services, decentralized and in microseconds, from periodically synchronized state; we add a domain compartment for the most sensitive data; and we quantify the cost of compliance against unconstrained and centralized references rather than proposing a placement heuristic. Learning-based privacy-aware offloading [@min2019privacy] protects information leaked *by the act of offloading*, and federated learning [@mcmahan2017fl] keeps training data local. We address a different point in the design space: per-request, decentralized routing of categorized inference with microsecond decisions and periodically synchronized state, and we compare against the decentralized and centralized references a practitioner would consider (round-robin, power-of-two choices, join-the-shortest-queue, static pools and a centralized dispatcher with perfect information).

**Admission control in inference serving.** Clockwork [@clockwork] and Nexus [@nexus] drop requests that can no longer meet their SLO instead of executing them late, because late work consumes capacity without contributing goodput. We find that the same principle is decisive for decentralized edge routing near saturation (Section VI-D).

**Energy-aware offloading.** Offloading with energy-harvesting devices has been studied with Lyapunov optimization [@mao2016harvest], and carbon-aware provisioning has been proposed for datacenter LLM serving [@ecoserve]. A natural extension of EPARA's handler is to scale the offload score by each peer's energy headroom. We test this hypothesis in Section VII.

**Load balancing with stale information.** Sending jobs to the apparently least-loaded server under stale information causes herd behaviour, whereas randomized choices degrade gracefully [@mitzenmacher2000old]; the power of two choices [@mitzenmacher2001two] and join-the-shortest-queue [@winston1977] are the standard references. EPARA computes idle goodput from periodically synchronized state, and we include both as trust-filtered baselines.

# III. System Model and Problem Formulation

## A. Edge servers, trust and energy

The system has a set $\mathcal{N}$ of $N$ edge servers. Server $j$ has $S_j$ GPU *slots* (MPS partitions, four per GPU), a *clearance level* $c_j\in\{0,1,2\}$ and an administrative *trust domain* $d_j$. Clearance captures the certification of the operator (e.g., public, certified, regulated); the domain captures ownership or jurisdiction. Network latency between servers $i$ and $j$ is $\ell_{ij}$. An optional cloud tier offers $S_\mathrm{cl}$ slots at one-way latency $\ell_\mathrm{cl}$; it has clearance $c_\mathrm{cl}=1$ (a certified provider) but belongs to no edge domain.

Energy is supplied at mean rate $\bar P_j=\kappa_j S_j P_s$, where $P_s$ is the power of one active slot and $\kappa_j$ the supply fraction. Supply is either constant or *solar*, $P_j(t)=\bar P_j\,(1+\sin(2\pi t/T_\mathrm{s}+\varphi_j))$, with phases $\varphi_j$ that differ across three time zones. Server $j$ holds an energy buffer $E_j(t)\le B_j$ that is refilled by the supply and charged $P_s$ per slot-second when a job is committed. We characterize aggregate scarcity by the *supply ratio*
$$\Phi=\frac{\sum_j \bar P_j}{\rho\sum_j S_j P_s},\qquad\qquad(1)$$
the mean supply divided by the power needed to serve the offered load $\rho$; $\Phi<1$ means that no router can serve all demand. We study two consequences of a drained buffer: (i) *soft throttling*: when $E_j/B_j<5\%$ a request runs 1.6$\times$ slower and a stream cannot start; and (ii) *hard outage*: when $E_j/B_j\le 1\%$ the server accepts no new work until it has recharged to 30%. In both models, jobs already admitted always run to completion: their energy is charged at admission, and a buffer may be overdrawn, which models a protected battery reserve that is repaid before the server recovers.

## B. Requests and categories

A request $r$ arrives at its *ingress* server $o_r$ at time $a_r$ for service $l_r$ and carries a *sensitivity* $s_r\in\{0,1,2\}$. Following EPARA, each service belongs to one of four categories: latency-sensitive (LS) or frequency-sensitive (FS), crossed with $<1$ or $>1$ GPU. Service $l$ occupies $m_l$ slots for $\tau_l$ seconds (for an FS stream, for the stream's duration). An LS request succeeds if it completes by $a_r+\mathrm{SLO}_l$; an FS stream succeeds if it starts within $\mathrm{SLO}_l$ and runs unthrottled.

## C. Trust constraint and threat model

Request $r$ may be *processed* by server $j$ only if
$$\mathrm{elig}(r,j)=\big[c_j\ge s_r\big]\wedge\big([s_r<2]\vee[d_j=d_{o_r}]\big).\qquad\qquad(2)$$
Sensitivity-1 data therefore requires a certified operator, and sensitivity-2 data must additionally stay within the ingress domain. Processing $r$ at an ineligible server is a *trust violation*. In lattice terms, (2) admits exactly the servers whose (clearance, domain) label dominates the request's label [@bell1973], [@denning1976].

*Processing versus relaying.* We distinguish processing, which requires the plaintext input, from relaying. The device encrypts the payload end-to-end for the processing server and sends only the routing metadata ($l_r$, $s_r$, $d_{o_r}$) in the clear; a server that merely forwards the request, including an ineligible ingress server, never sees the plaintext. Eligibility is therefore checked only where the request is processed. Deployments that cannot provide end-to-end encryption must instead let devices attach only to eligible ingress servers; this corresponds to full alignment ($\lambda=1$) in Section VI-C, where we show that offloading still produces violations unless the router enforces (2).

*Threat model.* Operators of ineligible servers are honest-but-curious: they forward correctly and do not collude to break encryption. Clearance and domain labels are bound to each server by remote attestation [@coker2011] (for example, a trusted execution environment [@costan2016sgx] reporting a measured software stack and a certificate issued by the domain's policy authority), and the routing state carries only attested labels. Compromised attestation, side channels, traffic analysis of metadata and misbehaving eligible servers are out of scope. Under these assumptions, a router that enforces (2) at the processing server cannot cause a violation, and a router that ignores it causes one whenever it processes a request at an ineligible server.

## D. Objective: goodput subject to compliance

Let $y_r\in\{0,1\}$ indicate that $r$ met its SLO and $v_r\in\{0,1\}$ that it was processed at an ineligible server. The routing problem is a constrained one:
$$\max\ G=\frac{1}{T}\sum_r y_r\quad\text{s.t.}\quad v_r=0\ \ \forall r.\qquad\qquad(3)$$
We compare routers that satisfy the constraint by construction on their SLO attainment $G\,T/|\mathcal R|$ and its *category-balanced* version, the mean of the four per-category values, so that the numerous short classification requests do not dominate. The *cost of compliance* is the gap between the raw SLO attainment of an unconstrained router and that of a compliant one; we measure it both for EPARA-style routing and for a centralized dispatcher with perfect information. Routers that ignore (2) are not feasible solutions of (3); we report them as references, using *compliant goodput* $G_c=\frac1T\sum_r y_r(1-v_r)$ only to show what such a router delivers lawfully.

## E. How large are violations without a trust filter?

The violation rate of a trust-oblivious router is set by the label distributions, not by its design. If a request is processed at its ingress server, and sensitivities are drawn independently of the ingress clearance,
$$V_\mathrm{loc}=\sum_{c} \Pr[c_{o}=c]\,\Pr[s_r>c]\qquad\qquad(4)$$
(no domain violation is possible at the ingress server). With clearances 0/1/2 w.p. 0.3/0.4/0.3 and sensitivities w.p. 0.60/0.24/0.16, $V_\mathrm{loc}=0.3\cdot0.40+0.4\cdot0.16=18.4\%$; a destination drawn uniformly at random gives $0.24\cdot0.3+0.16\cdot(1-0.3/3)=21.6\%$. We report the simulated violation rates of trust-oblivious routers only as a check of these numbers, not as a finding. Because real sensitive data tends to originate at high-clearance sites, we also let a fraction $\lambda$ of requests originate only at servers whose clearance covers their sensitivity; then $V_\mathrm{loc}$ falls to $(1-\lambda)V_\mathrm{loc}$, but offloaded requests can still violate (Section VI-C).


# IV. Design of TRACE

Fig. 1 summarizes the design. TRACE replaces EPARA's request handler; category profiles and service placement are taken as given. Each server $i$ keeps a view of every peer $j$: spare slots $\hat\sigma_{ij}$ over a horizon $\Delta=0.5$ s and the peer's online status, both taken from the last ring synchronization and debited locally by $i$'s own offloads since then.

## A. Trust-filtered, capacity-proportional offloading

On arrival at server $i$ (the ingress or a forwarding peer), $r$ is processed locally if $i$ is eligible, online, hosts $l_r$ and can meet the SLO. Otherwise $i$ samples a peer $j$ with probability proportional to
$$w_{ij}(r)=\mathrm{elig}(r,j)\cdot\phi(\hat\sigma_{ij},m_{l_r})\cdot\frac{1}{1+\ell_{ij}/\ell_0}\cdot\gamma_i^{\,\max(0,\,c_j-s_r)},\qquad\qquad(5)$$
where $\phi(\sigma,m)=\sigma$ if $\sigma\ge m$ and $0.05\,\sigma$ otherwise (prefer peers that fit), $\ell_0=20$ ms, and peers already visited or not hosting $l_r$ are excluded. A request is forwarded at most $K_{\max}=2$ times. Sampling rather than picking the arg-max keeps the router robust to stale views [@mitzenmacher2000old]. Without the last factor ($\gamma_i=1$), (5) is the *trust filter* (TF).

## B. Adaptive best-fit clearance

Non-sensitive requests that run on high-clearance servers consume the only capacity that sensitive requests may use. The last factor in (5), with $\gamma_i<1$, makes a request prefer the *least* clearance that suffices, the same fragmentation argument as best-fit bin packing. Best-fit only pays when sensitive traffic is common, and it costs SLO attainment when sensitive traffic is rare, because it concentrates non-sensitive load on low-clearance servers. TRACE therefore enables it adaptively: each server keeps an exponentially weighted estimate $\hat q_i$ of the share of sensitive requests arriving at it (weight 0.002 per request, a memory of a few seconds) and uses $\gamma_i=\gamma$ if $\hat q_i\ge q^\ast$ and $\gamma_i=1$ otherwise. Both $\gamma=0.3$ and $q^\ast=30\%$ are chosen on tuning seeds that are disjoint from the evaluation seeds (Section V).

## C. SLO-aware admission and cloud fallback

If no eligible peer remains, or $K_{\max}$ is reached, and the request cannot meet its SLO at the current server, TRACE sends it to the cloud if the cloud is eligible and can meet the SLO, and otherwise *sheds* it (rejects it immediately, so the client can retry or degrade). Executing such a request late would occupy slots without adding goodput and delay the requests queued behind it. Section VI-D shows that this rule, which we apply to every router in the main experiments for a fair comparison, removes the collapse of capacity-aware routing at saturation.

**Algorithm 1: TRACE request handling at server $i$** (input: request $r$, visited set $V$, forward count $k$)

1. If $\mathrm{elig}(r,i)$, $i$ is online, hosts $l_r$ and the SLO is feasible at $i$: process $r$ at $i$ and return.
2. If $k<K_{\max}$: compute $w_{ij}(r)$ by (5) for all $j\notin V$; if $\sum_j w_{ij}>0$, sample $j\sim w_{ij}/\sum_{j'}w_{ij'}$, debit $\hat\sigma_{ij}$, forward $r$ to $j$ with $V\cup\{j\}$ and $k+1$, and return.
3. If the cloud is eligible for $r$ and can meet the SLO: send $r$ to the cloud and return.
4. Shed $r$.

**Complexity and state.** One decision costs $O(N)$ arithmetic on the local view and no communication. The synchronized state per peer is a few bytes (spare slots, online flag, backlog estimate); clearance and domain labels are static and exchanged once, at attestation. A ring synchronization therefore moves $O(N)$ bytes per server per period, about 1.2 kB per hop for $N=120$ and $H=1$ s. Its *latency*, not its volume, limits scale: one circulation of the ring takes $N$ hops, roughly 1.5 s for $N=120$ servers with our latency model, so views become older as $N$ grows. Our simulator synchronizes all views instantaneously every $H$ seconds and studies staleness through $H$ (Section VI-E); for large $N$, gossip or tree-based dissemination would be needed.

## D. Energy-aware variants (hypotheses tested in Section VII)

To test whether the offload score should account for energy headroom $\hat h_{ij}=E_j/B_j$ (from the last synchronization), we evaluate five extensions of TF: **EW** multiplies (5) by $\hat h_{ij}^{\beta}$ ($\beta=1$) and hands a request off a server whose own headroom is below $\theta=0.25$ when its SLO slack exceeds 50 ms and a peer with headroom above 0.5 fits; **EB** uses a barrier weight $\min\{1,\max(0.05,\hat h_{ij}/0.3)\}$; **EC** treats energy as a capacity dimension, replacing $\hat\sigma_{ij}$ by $\min(\hat\sigma_{ij},\hat e_{ij})$, where $\hat e_{ij}$ is the number of slots that $j$'s energy can power over the horizon; **EW-bounded** multiplies (5) by $(1+\hat h_{ij})/2\in[0.5,1]$, so that energy only deprioritizes and never excludes a peer, and does no hand-off; and **EF** is a feasibility oracle that uses the *true, current* buffer of every server and excludes a server (including the local one) only if it cannot power the job without throttling or outage, with no preference among feasible servers.

# V. Evaluation Methodology

**Simulator.** We extended our event-driven simulator (Python/NumPy). It executes the full routing logic for every request but abstracts computation and transmission by service-time and latency tables, the methodology EPARA uses for its large-scale study [@epara2025]. EPARA's operators are not modelled: each category has a fixed slot count and service time after operator selection, so batching gains and co-location interference are not captured. Unless stated otherwise every server hosts every service (full replication); Section VI-F also evaluates partial placement. All parameters (Table II) are *assumptions* modelled on EPARA's P100 testbed scale; they are not measurements, and absolute numbers should not be compared with EPARA's. The simulator, experiment scripts and raw results are available at https://github.com/gaurav3611/edge-cloud-ai-routing.

**Separating trust from energy.** In the first version of this study, every experiment used energy-capped servers with a mean supply below the offered load, so energy scarcity confounded the trust results. In this version, the trust experiments (Section VI) run with unconstrained energy, and energy is studied separately and explicitly in Section VII, where we control the supply ratio $\Phi$ of Eq. (1).

**Load.** Offered load $\rho$ is the demanded slot-seconds per second divided by the total number of slots. Shares of demand are measured in slot-seconds; by request count, classification requests are about 99% of all requests, which is why we report the category-balanced metric.

**Routers.** *Trust-oblivious references:* local only; decentralized round-robin (RR), an InterEdge-style baseline [@interedge]; EPARA-style capacity-proportional offloading, i.e., (5) without the trust and best-fit factors; and a *centralized oracle* that dispatches every request with perfect, instantaneous knowledge of every server's slot occupancy to the server with the earliest finish time (no hop limit). *Compliant routers:* TF; TRACE; TF-RR (round-robin over eligible peers); TF-P2C (power of two choices [@mitzenmacher2001two]: two eligible peers sampled uniformly, forward to the one with the smaller synchronized backlog); TF-JSQ (join-the-shortest-queue [@winston1977]: the eligible peer with the smallest synchronized backlog plus latency); *static pools*, the partitioning a practitioner would deploy, in which a request may only be processed by servers whose clearance *equals* its sensitivity (and, for sensitivity 2, within its domain), with capacity-proportional offloading inside the pool; and the *centralized oracle with trust*, which restricts the oracle to eligible servers and, among those that meet the SLO, prefers the least sufficient clearance. The oracles are strong references, not proven optima: greedy earliest-finish dispatch has no look-ahead. All routers share the same admission rule (Section IV-C), category profiles, topology and arrival sequence for a given seed.

**Metrics.** We report raw and compliant SLO attainment (per request and category-balanced), compliant goodput, violation rate, forwards and shed requests per request and routing time per decision. For energy we also report throttled fraction, server availability (outage model), the coefficient of variation (CV) of per-server utilization and availability, and energy per successful request.

**Seeds, run length and statistics.** Every trust experiment uses 30 evaluation seeds (20 for the staleness and placement studies, 10 for parameter sensitivity and scale); each seed draws a new topology and arrival sequence. Runs last 60 s with a 10 s warm-up, covering three 20 s burst cycles. Energy experiments use 10 seeds and 180 s runs with a 60 s warm-up and random initial charge, so that measurements start after the initial transient. $\gamma$ and $q^\ast$ were chosen on 10 separate tuning seeds (1001–1010) that are never used for evaluation. Values are means with 95% Student-t confidence intervals over seeds. Differences are paired over seeds and tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test; we declare a family of tests for every experiment and correct within it with Holm's method [@holm1979], reporting Holm-adjusted $p$-values ($p_H$). With 30 seeds the smallest attainable two-sided $p$ is about $2\times10^{-9}$, so corrected tests have power. To separate topology from workload effects, a variance-decomposition experiment runs 5 arrival sequences on each of 10 topologies.
