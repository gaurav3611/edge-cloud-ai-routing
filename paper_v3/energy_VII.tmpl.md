# VII. Energy-Aware Routing: A Hypothesis Test

**Hypothesis.** On power-capped, solar or battery-backed sites, scaling the offload score by peers' energy headroom should raise compliant SLO attainment, because it steers work away from servers that are about to throttle or go offline. We test this hypothesis with the five variants of Section IV-D on top of TF, under both energy models, with constant supply at $\Phi\in\{0.6,0.9,1.2\}$ and with phase-shifted solar supply at $\Phi\in\{0.9,1.2\}$, in 180 s runs measured after a 60 s warm-up from a random initial charge (10 seeds; Tables VII and VIII, Fig. 7). For each experiment, the 30 (constant supply) or 20 (solar) comparisons with TF form one Holm family.

**Energy scarcity with soft throttling.** When supply is well below demand ($\Phi=0.6$), steering by energy hurts. TF+EW loses {{a:B_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.6 outage=False}} points and TF+EC {{a:B_energy|TF+EC - TF phi=0.6 outage=False}} points against TF ({{v:B|0.6|False|tf|cslo}}), while the bounded variant that only deprioritizes is indistinguishable from TF ({{d:B_energy|TF+EW-bounded - TF phi=0.6 outage=False}}). The losses are consistent across seeds (TF+EW and TF+EC lose on every seed) but, with 10 seeds and 30 tests in the family, their Holm-adjusted $p$-values are {{ph:B_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.6 outage=False}} and {{ph:B_energy|TF+EC - TF phi=0.6 outage=False}}. The mechanism is visible in the measured dispersion: TF+EW raises the coefficient of variation of per-server utilization from {{v:B|0.6|False|tf|util_cv}} to {{v:B|0.6|False|ew|util_cv}} and forwards per request from {{v:B|0.6|False|tf|hops}} to {{v:B|0.6|False|ew|hops}}, and TF+EC raises the utilization CV to {{v:B|0.6|False|ec|util_cv}}. Work is concentrated on the servers that currently hold energy, while servers with low headroom but idle slots, which could still run short jobs, are avoided. Energy weighting does reduce throttling (throttled fraction {{v:B|0.6|False|ew|throttled}} instead of {{v:B|0.6|False|tf|throttled}}), but the extra forwarding and concentration cost more SLO attainment than the avoided throttling saves. Even perfect energy information does not help here: the feasibility oracle TF+EF loses {{a:B_energy|TF+EF (oracle feas.) - TF phi=0.6 outage=False}} points, because excluding servers that cannot finish a job without throttling also excludes capacity that would have served the job within its SLO.

**Hard outages.** Under the outage model the sign changes. TF+EW is ahead of TF at every supply ratio ({{d:B_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.6 outage=True}}, {{d:B_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.9 outage=True}} and {{d:B_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=1.2 outage=True}} points at $\Phi=0.6$, 0.9 and 1.2) and keeps more servers online: at $\Phi=0.9$, availability rises from {{v:B|0.9|True|tf|avail}} to {{v:B|0.9|True|ew|avail}} and drain events fall from {{v:B|0.9|True|tf|drains}} to {{v:B|0.9|True|ew|drains}} per minute. Avoiding peers that are close to their outage threshold prevents the outage itself, which removes a whole server's capacity for the recharge period. The gains are small, however, and none is significant after correction (Holm $p\ge${{phmin:B_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.6 outage=True;TF+EW - TF phi=0.9 outage=True;TF+EW - TF phi=1.2 outage=True}}). TF+EC coincides with TF under this model, because its energy capacity binds only when a buffer is nearly empty or overdrawn, and such servers are already offline.

**Sufficient and heterogeneous supply.** When aggregate supply exceeds demand ($\Phi=1.2$), with constant or phase-shifted solar supply, every variant is within {{maxabs:B_energy,S_energy|phi=1.2}} points of TF, and no difference is significant. With solar supply at $\Phi=0.9$, so that some sites are in surplus while others are in deficit, the picture is the same as with constant supply but weaker: TF+EW loses {{a:S_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.9 outage=False}} points under soft throttling and gains {{d:S_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.9 outage=True}} points under outage, neither significant after correction. Shifting work toward sites that currently have surplus energy, which is the scenario in which energy-aware routing seemed most promising, therefore yields no measurable benefit at the per-request timescale in our setting.

**Timescales and staleness.** With a short 20 s buffer the soft-throttling penalty of TF+EW remains ({{d:U_buffer|TF+EW - TF buffer=20.0s outage=False}} points) and the outage gain is {{d:U_buffer|TF+EW - TF buffer=20.0s outage=True}} points; with a 600 s buffer, which the load in our runs never drains, all variants coincide with TF (within {{maxabs:U_buffer|buffer=600.0s}} points). Under solar supply at $\Phi=0.9$ with outages, the difference between TF+EW and TF is {{g:0.1|ew}} points with fresh state ($H=0.1$ s) and {{g:2.0|ew}} points with stale state ($H=2$ s), so staleness does not change the conclusion.

**Conclusion of the test.** The hypothesis is not supported as a general rule. Energy-headroom weighting is harmful when energy is scarce and an empty buffer slows work down, gives small and statistically unconfirmed gains when an empty buffer takes a server offline, and is neutral when supply is sufficient. A bounded weight that never excludes a peer is harmless in every regime but also never helps significantly. At the per-request timescale, energy information is best used as a feasibility check at the processing server and, if hard outages are expected, as a mild preference against peers near their outage threshold; larger gains, if any, are more likely at slower layers that can act on forecasts, such as service placement, admission control and DVFS.
