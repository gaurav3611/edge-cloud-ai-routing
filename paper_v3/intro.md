# I. Introduction

Inference workloads are migrating from central data centers toward the network edge. Applications such as video analytics, voice assistants and robot control expect answers within tens to hundreds of milliseconds, and the inputs they consume, including camera frames, physiological signals and speech, increasingly fall under privacy regulation. Edge-cloud collaborative computing (ECCC) responds with a three-tier arrangement: edge servers close to users carry most of the load and hand work to peers or to the cloud when they run out of capacity [@liu2025eccc]. Any single edge server is small, and the fleet is heterogeneous, yet the combined idle capacity of many servers is large, provided that requests can be steered to it quickly.

EPARA [@epara2025] is a recent design that exemplifies this approach. It sorts inference services along two dimensions, latency- versus frequency-sensitive and below versus above one GPU, and picks a parallelization strategy for each class. A decentralized request handler then keeps a request local or passes it to a peer with probability proportional to that peer's idle goodput, while a periodic placement step moves models toward demand. In EPARA's evaluation this combination substantially outperforms earlier edge and datacenter serving systems [@epara2025].

The handler, like those of the systems EPARA is compared with [@interedge], [@galaxy], [@servp], [@alpaserve], [@usher], regards every server that hosts a model as a legitimate destination for every request. That assumption often fails in practice. Imaging data from a hospital may be processed only by certified operators, and residency rules can confine data to one organization or jurisdiction [@gdpr], [@hummel2021]. A router that disregards such rules can report high goodput while part of what it serves is served unlawfully. A second implicit assumption concerns energy: many edge sites are power-capped, solar-powered or battery-backed, and steering work toward servers with spare energy, as energy-aware offloading does for mobile devices [@mao2016harvest], looks like an obvious refinement.

We therefore treat request routing as a *constrained* problem: maximize timely service subject to the requirement that no request is processed outside its trust boundary. Two questions follow. *How much timely service does compliance cost a fast decentralized router, measured against unconstrained and centralized references, and which routing rules keep that cost low?* And *does making the offload decision energy-aware help, and under what supply conditions?*

Our contributions are the following.

- **Model.** We formulate trust-constrained routing for categorized edge inference using multilevel-security labels (clearance and trust domain), state the threat model, including how ingress servers relay data they may not process, and give a closed form for the violation rate of trust-oblivious routing, which shows that this rate is fixed by the label distributions rather than by the router (Section III).
- **Design.** TRACE extends capacity-proportional offloading with a hard trust filter, a best-fit clearance preference that each server switches on when it observes enough sensitive traffic, and SLO-aware admission that sheds or sends to the cloud requests that can no longer meet their deadline. A decision takes tens of microseconds of local computation (Section IV).
- **Evaluation against strong references.** We compare TRACE with trust-filtered round-robin, power-of-two-choices and join-the-shortest-queue routing, static per-tier pools and a centralized dispatcher with perfect information, over 30 evaluation seeds with Holm-corrected paired tests, and we vary the correlation between data sensitivity and ingress clearance, the share of sensitive traffic, overload, state staleness, placement, a cloud fallback, topology and every routing parameter (Section VI).
- **Energy-aware routing as a tested hypothesis.** We evaluate five energy-aware variants, including a feasibility oracle with perfect energy information, across supply ratios below and above demand, constant and phase-shifted solar supply, and short and long battery buffers, and we measure the mechanisms (load dispersion, availability, forwarding) directly (Section VII).

All results come from simulation with documented, assumed parameters at the scale of EPARA's testbed. They are not hardware measurements and should not be compared in absolute terms with EPARA's reported numbers.
