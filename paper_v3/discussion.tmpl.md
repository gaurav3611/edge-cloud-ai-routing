# VIII. Discussion

**What the results say about compliant routing.** Three design elements matter, in decreasing order of effect. First, SLO-aware admission decides whether a router survives saturation at all: without it, every router we evaluated, including a centralized oracle, served fewer than a quarter of requests on time at $\rho=1.0$. Second, the trust filter is a precondition, not an optimization: a router that ignores the constraint violates it for a share of requests that is fixed by the label distributions, and aligning data with clearance only reduces that share. Third, among compliant routers the choice of peer matters most near saturation, where capacity-proportional sampling outperforms round-robin, power-of-two choices and join-the-shortest-queue; below saturation, TF-JSQ is equally good. Best-fit clearance is a modest refinement worth about one to two points when a substantial share of traffic is sensitive, and the adaptive rule makes it safe to leave enabled. We therefore regard the trust filter with SLO-aware admission as the core of TRACE and best-fit clearance as an optional component.

**Cost of compliance.** With perfect information, compliance costs 1.2 points of SLO attainment at the default sensitive share and up to 10 points when 80% of the traffic is sensitive; at the default share, a decentralized router pays about twice as much (2.7 points). The cost is driven by the capacity that is eligible for the most restrictive class, so it is a property of the deployment (how many servers carry which clearance in which domain) as much as of the router. Trust-aware service placement, which we hold fixed, is the natural lever to reduce it further.

**Static partitioning.** Dedicated pools per sensitivity tier are simple to audit, and they match TRACE when the pools are sized to the traffic, as they happen to be in our setting at 60–80% sensitive traffic. They fail badly when the traffic mix and the pool sizes diverge (0.16 to 0.67 C-SLO at 0–40% sensitive traffic). Routing that lets less sensitive requests use idle higher-clearance capacity removes this sizing problem.

**Implications for EPARA-style systems.** The trust filter is a small, local change to EPARA's handler: a per-peer eligibility test on static, attested labels, with decisions in tens of microseconds and no extra communication. SLO-aware admission is a local check as well. Because the violation rate of a trust-oblivious handler is set by the deployment rather than by the router, we argue that edge inference systems that handle regulated data should report goodput subject to compliance, together with the cost of compliance against a reference, rather than raw goodput alone.

**Energy awareness.** Energy-headroom weighting did not hold up as a general improvement (Section VII). Every policy draws on the same energy income, so a router cannot create energy; it can only decide where work runs. When an empty buffer merely slows work down, avoiding low-headroom peers concentrates load and adds forwarding, which costs more than the throttling it prevents ({{a:B_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.6 outage=False}} points at $\Phi=0.6$). When an empty buffer takes a server offline, a mild preference against nearly drained peers prevents outages and slightly raises SLO attainment ({{d:B_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.9 outage=True}} points at $\Phi=0.9$, not significant after correction). Whether energy-aware routing helps therefore depends on how a site fails when energy runs out, which should be measured before such a policy is deployed.

**Threats to validity and limitations.** *Simulation only.* All results come from an event-driven simulator. Service times, slot power, label distributions, energy supply and the network model are assumptions at the scale of EPARA's testbed, not measurements, and absolute values should not be compared with EPARA's results [@epara2025]. *Operator abstraction.* EPARA's operators are reduced to fixed per-category slot and time profiles; batching gains, co-location interference and model loading are not modelled, so the evaluation concerns the request-handling layer only. *Workload.* Arrivals are synthetic (Poisson with hot spots and bursts), not the Azure function and LLM traces used by EPARA [@epara2025], [@shahrad2020serverless]. *Fixed layers.* Placement is either full replication or random partial replication; demand- and trust-aware placement is not evaluated. *State dissemination.* Views are synchronized instantaneously every $H$ seconds; the propagation delay of a ring, which grows with $N$, and the bandwidth of synchronization are not modelled. *Trust model.* Labels are static, attested and honest; eligibility follows a single rule (2), whereas real policies (consent, purpose limitation, cross-border transfer rules) are richer. *References.* The centralized oracles are greedy earliest-finish dispatchers with perfect information, not proven optima; they are not upper bounds in overload. *Energy model.* Energy is a buffer with fixed per-slot power; DVFS, idle power and cooling are not modelled, and solar supply is a compressed sinusoid rather than a measured profile. *Statistics.* Trust experiments use 30 seeds (fewer for robustness and scale studies) and energy experiments 10 seeds; all claims rest on paired tests with Holm correction within declared families, and $\gamma$ and $q^\ast$ were tuned on separate seeds. *Timing.* Decision times come from a Python prototype on a shared machine and are indicative only.

# IX. Conclusion

Decentralized, capacity-proportional request routing pools the scattered capacity of edge servers effectively, but routers designed this way treat every server as a lawful destination. We formulated trust-constrained routing for categorized edge inference as goodput maximization subject to zero violations, using multilevel-security labels and an explicit threat model, and showed that the violation rate of trust-oblivious routing is fixed by the label distributions. TRACE combines a hard trust filter, SLO-aware admission and an adaptive best-fit clearance preference with capacity-proportional offloading. Against trust-filtered round-robin, power-of-two-choices and join-the-shortest-queue routing, static pools and a centralized oracle, TRACE was the best or tied-best decentralized compliant router at every load (tied with join-the-shortest-queue below saturation), within about three points of the centralized oracle below saturation and ahead of it in overload; compliance cost 1.2 points with perfect information and 2.7 points for decentralized routing at the default sensitive share. SLO-aware admission was the single most important element, raising every router's SLO attainment at $\rho=1.0$ by more than 50 points. Energy-headroom weighting, which we had expected to help on energy-constrained sites, did not hold up as a general rule: it lost {{a:B_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.6 outage=False}} points when energy was scarce and an empty buffer throttled work, gained at most {{d:B_energy|TF+EW - TF phi=0.6 outage=True}} points (not significant after correction) when an empty buffer caused an outage, and made no difference when supply was sufficient.

The next steps are to replace the assumed service and power profiles with measurements on edge hardware, to drive the simulator with production traces, to model state dissemination at scale, to co-design trust-aware service placement with the router, and to study energy awareness at the placement and admission-control layers, where forecasts can be used.
